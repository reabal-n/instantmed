# Earn premium trust through restraint and proof

The landing pages that read as high-production in 2025–26 follow one recipe, whether they are Stripe, Linear and Mercury or Hims, Numan and Australia's NextClinic. The recipe is a 3–8-word headline, one supporting sentence, one action that names the next step and its price, and a hero visual that carries information. For a health service, that visual is the thing the patient receives. The premium signal sits in execution: one type family set with care, a warm neutral ground with one owned accent, exact alignment, and short motion that does a job. The effects most saved on 21st.dev and the telehealth concepts most viewed on Dribbble (scroll-hijacking heroes, glass, orbital timelines, GLP-1 pen packshots, AI portraits) are the patterns 2026 critics now list as AI slop, and several would be unlawful in Australia. The measured evidence is strongest where InstantMed needs it most. Healthcare pages written at a 5th–7th-grade reading level have a **10.8% median conversion rate**, and harder text performs 48–56% worse ([Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/healthcare-wellness-conversion-rate/)). **74% of viewing time** falls in the first two screens ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). A **0.1 s** mobile speed gain lifted retail conversion 8.4% ([web.dev](https://web.dev/case-studies/milliseconds-make-millions)). High extra costs are the **most common reason** shoppers abandon a purchase ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate)). The evidence on visual style is weaker: it is mostly observation and practitioner opinion, and Mobbin blocked access outright. In GoodUI's database of 642 tests, most UI changes produced no significant win ([GoodUI](https://goodui.org/patterns/)), so each stage of a redesign needs its own measured read. Ahpra's ban on clinical testimonials, the TGA's June 2026 guidance on implied medicines and InstantMed's own rules against fake people and named doctors remove the global DTC toolkit. What they leave is what the conversion evidence favours anyway: process transparency, specimen artefacts, regulator facts, eligibility limits and refund-on-decline stated as policy. InstantMed's bigger risks are internal. Its design system still ships several recognised template tells. Its display face is the one NextClinic uses for its H1. Its **A$24.95** single-day certificate costs more than every certificate entry price observed in the Australian teardown, except Doctors on Demand's after-hours consultation. Visible quality therefore has to justify a price premium that Ahpra's rules stop it defending through price comparison.

## Premium is judged in 50 milliseconds, and health visitors judge design first

First impressions form in **17–50 ms**. Google's research found that people strongly prefer designs that look both simple and familiar, and that layouts which break expectations start behind and stay there ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/users-love-simple-and-familiar-designs-why-websites-need-to-make-a-great-first-impression/)). Attractive interfaces are also judged more usable than they are. In Kurosu and Kashimura's study of 26 ATM layouts with 252 participants, perceived usability tracked aesthetics more closely than actual usability. NN/g adds that polish cannot rescue a broken flow ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/aesthetic-usability-effect/)).

In health the stakes are higher. Sillence and colleagues followed 15 women researching a health decision over four weeks. Design appeal drove which sites they rejected at first pass. The credibility and personalisation of the content then decided which of the remaining sites they trusted ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/985692.985776)). A secondary summary attributes **83%** of comments on rejected sites to design factors, although that figure has not been checked against the paper ([The Team W](https://www.blog.theteamw.com/2011/03/06/100-things-you-should-know-about-people-73-1st-screening-about-trusting-a-website-is-based-on-the-look-and-feel-2/)). NN/g names four stable credibility factors: design quality, upfront disclosure of costs and policies, comprehensive and current content, and presence on the wider web. It also notes that typos and broken links erode credibility quickly ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/)). For a telehealth page, design decides whether a visitor stays long enough for the doctors, the process and the price to make their case.

"Awwwards-level" is a craft standard with no demonstrated link to conversion. Awwwards weights Design at 40%, Usability 30%, Creativity 20% and Content 10%, and at least 18 jurors score each site ([Awwwards](https://www.awwwards.com/about-evaluation/)). The research found no study linking these scores to conversion, and galleries such as Godly and Land-book publish no criteria at all. What transfers is the execution the best product companies have documented. Stripe rebuilt its palette in CIELAB after an audit found that almost none of its small-text colours met contrast, and a rule about how many palette steps separate two colours now guarantees 4.5:1 for small text ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/blog/accessible-color-systems)). Linear sets headings in Inter Display and body text in Inter. It moved its colour system to LCH, cut each theme from 98 variables to 3, and reduced its blue accents for a more neutral look ([Linear](https://linear.app/now/how-we-redesigned-the-linear-ui)). Mercury sets its custom Arcadia headlines at a variable weight of 480 with a body line height of 1.625, although two third-party analyses disagree on its type sizes ([Blake Crosley](https://blakecrosley.com/guides/design/mercury); [DesignMD](https://designmd.cc/benchmarks/mercury)). Across 100 developer-tool pages, Evil Martians found that the dominant hero is centred in a max-width container with a visual directly below the headline, and that simple, well-made pages beat flashy ones ([Evil Martians](https://evilmartians.com/chronicles/we-studied-100-devtool-landing-pages-here-is-what-actually-works-in-2025)). Given the familiarity finding, InstantMed should express quality through type, colour discipline, art direction and alignment. An unusual page structure would cost it trust.

Component marketplaces pull the other way. 21st.dev lists more than 12,000 components, and its most-engaged featured items are showpiece effects ([21st.dev](https://21st.dev/community/components)):

| Component | Engagement count |
|---|---|
| Scroll-expanding media hero | 10.0k |
| Aceternity's container-scroll animation | 9.6k |
| Spline 3D scene | 8.9k |
| Spotlight card | 8.1k |
| Radial orbital timeline | 7.0k |
| Liquid-glass button | 6.0k |

The site does not label what these counts measure. Its own editorial guidance from August 2026 is far more restrained. If a hero must move, it says to use CSS or canvas. Autoplay video above the fold competes with the page's JavaScript in the critical first second, and scroll hijacking makes a page feel broken on a trackpad. Pages should preload one typeface, because the hero is where font swaps show ([21st.dev](https://21st.dev/blog/react-hero-section-examples)).

Tailark's teardown of its own best blocks shows where the polish actually comes from. Images are masked into the page instead of hard-cropped, and every border, shadow and gradient is drawn from theme tokens. The product is rendered as real HTML instead of a screenshot, and decorative light appears only at large breakpoints. The header condenses to one CTA on scroll, and a single orchestrated text reveal does the animation work ([Tailark](https://pro.tailark.com/blog/7-best-shadcn-hero-section-blocks-for-saas-websites)). Saves measure what developers admire; they say nothing about what converts.

The idea that does transfer from Stripe and Linear is that the product is the picture. A service with no dashboard to show can use its outcome instead: a specimen certificate, an eScript token arriving by SMS, or the first question of the form. NN/g's eyetracking shows that people study images that carry information and skip generic, feel-good imagery ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/photos-as-web-content/)). In an older Unbounce test, removing a decorative stock photo from above a form raised submissions **24%** ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/5-landing-page-a/b-tests-and-their-surprising-results)). None of these outcome visuals needs a face or a medicine.

## Winning heroes fit what, how much and what next into one glance

Global health leaders keep the first mobile screen to about **30–55 words**; One Medical is the outlier at 88. Most Australian certificate and prescription pages run **60–85 words**, and Hola reaches 146–169 once its discount pop-ups stack up ([Numan](https://www.numan.com/); [One Medical](https://www.onemedical.com/); [NextClinic](https://nextclinic.com.au/medical-certificates-online); [Hola](https://hola.health/)). Heavier screens are rarely richer. The extra words come from stacked promotions, SEO paragraphs and consent overlays. For a new Australian visitor, the consent overlay covered about half of InstantScripts' first screen and all of Medmate's ([InstantScripts](https://www.instantscripts.com.au/); [Medmate](https://medmate.com.au/)). Pilot adds a 294 px sticky banner about its move into the Hims & Hers group ([Pilot](https://www.pilot.com.au/)). Before the current plan, InstantMed's heroes measured about 28 words on the homepage, 60 on the certificate page and 73 on the prescriptions page ([InstantMed hero plan](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed-worktrees/landing-premium-plan/docs/superpowers/plans/2026-09-26-landing-premium-heroes.md)).

**The headline should name what the visitor came for.** People scanning headings and links take in roughly the first two words. In NN/g's test, a link that opened with a concrete label scored 85% comprehension from its first 11 characters; one that opened with a generic word scored 0% ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/first-2-words-a-signal-for-scanning/)). In an older Unbounce test, rewriting a headline and subhead to match the ad that brought the visitor raised leads **115%**, even though the matched copy was weaker on its own ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/5-landing-page-a/b-tests-and-their-surprising-results)). Julian Shapiro's test is that someone who reads only the header should know exactly what is for sale ([Julian Shapiro](https://www.julian.com/guide/growth/landing-pages)). Copyhackers' awareness model puts a person searching for a certificate or a repeat script among the most aware buyers, who need the deal (what it is, what it costs, the terms) rather than persuasion ([Copyhackers](https://copyhackers.com/2014/07/long-pages/)).

Australian challengers add warmth with situational headlines about being too sick for work or feeling rough ([NextClinic](https://nextclinic.com.au/medical-certificates-online); [Doccy](https://doccy.com.au/medical-certificate)). These read as more human than the keyword-stuffed headings on Qoctor, MIDOC and Hola, which feel templated ([Qoctor](https://www.qoctor.com.au/medical-certificate/); [MIDOC](https://www.midoc.com.au/)). The evidence supports combining the two: open with the category noun the visitor searched for, and put the situational warmth in the supporting line, section headings and microcopy.

Reading level matters more than wit. Across 41,000 pages, text at a 5th–7th-grade level converted at 11.1%, against 5.3% for professional-level writing. Difficult words, reading time and word count all correlated with lower conversion ([Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/)). In healthcare the median conversion rates are 5.1% overall and 5.3% for medical treatment ([Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/healthcare-wellness-conversion-rate/)).

**The action should say what happens next and what it costs.** NN/g watched generic get-started buttons pull users into sign-up flows when they expected to learn how a service worked, and summed up the lesson in four words: "A link is a promise" ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/get-started/)). Evil Martians found that most leading pages pair one bold primary action with a lighter secondary. It recommends labels specific to the action ([Evil Martians](https://evilmartians.com/chronicles/we-studied-100-devtool-landing-pages-here-is-what-actually-works-in-2025)), and Mercury pairs opening an account with trying a demo ([Mercury](https://mercury.com)).

The price belongs inside the action or right beside it. Of people who abandoned a purchase, 40% cited high extra costs and 12% could not see the total up front ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate)), and Ahpra lists unclear pricing as a breach ([Ahpra](https://www.ahpra.gov.au/Resources/Advertising-hub/Advertising-guidelines-and-other-guidance/Advertising-guidelines.aspx)). InstantMed's DESIGN.md already requires the price in the primary CTA on every service page ([DESIGN.md](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed-worktrees/landing-premium-plan/DESIGN.md)). The evidence on one versus several hero CTAs is weak: the widely shared statistics favouring a single CTA trace back to no primary study ([Flint](https://www.flint.com/blog/landing-page-cta-button-performance-statistics)).

**Proof belongs in or directly under the first screen, and the CTA's position depends on how complex the offer is.** NN/g's 2018 eyetracking put 57% of viewing time above the fold. It advises reserving the top of the page for the main business and user goals, and avoiding false floors where a page looks finished before it is ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)). Regulated brands put their disclosures in that zone. Mercury's partner-bank disclosure sits directly under the hero. So do Monzo's consumer-group badge, deposit-protection mark and eligibility line. Numan's regulator band appears in the first scroll ([Mercury](https://mercury.com); [Monzo](https://monzo.com); [Numan](https://www.numan.com/)).

In an older ContentVerve test, moving the CTA below the fold on a complex offer lifted conversion 304%, although other elements changed at the same time ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/5-landing-page-a/b-tests-and-their-surprising-results)). A single-day certificate and a repeat script are simple offers, so their action belongs in the first screen. The weight, ED and hair pathways are closer to the complex case, where explanation before the ask is justified.

**The visual should show the outcome in high fidelity, without delaying the headline.** The strongest Australian hero is NextClinic's card. It holds the headline, one 16–26-word line, two actions and a 2×2 grid of checkable operating facts, including a refund if the request is not approved, and it needs no testimonial ([NextClinic](https://nextclinic.com.au/)). Phone mock-ups are common locally, and what separates good ones from bad is fidelity and content. Qoctor's stock doctor inside a phone frame reads as dated. NextClinic's SMS eScript mock-up, which names no medicine, reads as current ([Qoctor](https://www.qoctor.com.au/); [NextClinic](https://nextclinic.com.au/prescriptions-online)). Tailark's techniques show how to make such a scene crisp: real HTML, masking, and a media aspect ratio that switches from portrait on phones to landscape on desktop ([Tailark](https://pro.tailark.com/blog/7-best-shadcn-hero-section-blocks-for-saas-websites)).

The visual has to stay light, and it must never hold the headline back. Chrome does not count paints at zero opacity as LCP candidates. An H1 that fades in from invisible therefore records its LCP at a later repaint ([Chromium](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/refs/heads/main/docs/speed/metrics_changelog/lcp.md); [DebugBear](https://www.debugbear.com/blog/opacity-animation-poor-lcp)).

In Unbounce's healthcare data, phones bring seven times the traffic of desktop, while desktop converts 22% better ([Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/healthcare-wellness-conversion-rate/)). On a phone the hero should run from headline to supporting sentence, action and proof. The visual comes last, cropped so it peeks above the bottom edge as a cue to scroll. Tap targets should be at least 44 px, and inputs at least 16 px so iOS does not zoom ([Vercel](https://vercel.com/design/guidelines)). A 343 px text column at 16–18 px gives about 35–45 characters per line, under the bottom of Butterick's 45–90-character range ([Practical Typography](https://practicaltypography.com/summary-of-key-rules.html)). Centred paragraphs longer than about two lines should go.

## Every section below the hero should retire one objection

The health pages studied share a stable arc. A hero leads into a service chooser or proof strip. A short how-it-works follows, then what you get and what it costs, safety and credentials, an FAQ, and a close with a single button.

**Homepages act as routers.** Numan's homepage runs from the hero to six service cards, a regulator and rating band, its clinical process, clinician profiles and an app block. It has **no pricing, no steps and no FAQ** ([Numan](https://www.numan.com/)). Pilot adds three steps (quiz, consult, delivery), a practitioner section, HealthDirect and ISO 27001 logos, and a LegitScript seal in the footer ([Pilot](https://www.pilot.com.au/)). None of the premium fintech and software homepages studied has an FAQ ([Mercury](https://mercury.com); [Stripe](https://stripe.com)).

**Service pages carry the detail.** NextClinic's certificate page is the most useful local template. It opens with a hero and fact grid, then tiered prices charged only if the request is approved. Three steps follow, ending with a line saying you are not charged if declined. After them come a certificate preview, a good-for and not-for block, an app block, reviews, a long FAQ and an emotional close. Within three scrolls the page has answered price, process, legitimacy and scope ([NextClinic](https://nextclinic.com.au/medical-certificates-online)).

Mobbin catalogues Hims' web assessment start and Headspace's onboarding from landing page to payment, but it returned HTTP 403 in every session, so its screens could not be compared ([Mobbin](https://mobbin.com/explore/web/app-categories/medical)). The DTC sites that could be reached show the same mobile skeleton: condition tiles near the top, three steps each led by a verb, a trust band, an FAQ accordion, and a CTA that leads into an assessment ([Nurx](https://www.nurx.com/)).

**How it works should be a short, real sequence.** Use a vertical ordered list of three or four steps. Each step names the action, who does it and what comes out, and the CTA follows the last step directly, as it does on Pilot and Nurx ([Pilot](https://www.pilot.com.au/); [Nurx](https://www.nurx.com/)). 21st.dev recommends marking up process timelines as ordered lists with decorative connecting lines, keeping to three to six items, and avoiding orbital layouts, which are poor at showing order ([21st.dev](https://21st.dev/blog/react-timeline-components)). One critic lists numbered 1-2-3 sequences as a template tell ([Developers Digest](https://www.developersdigest.tech/blog/ai-design-slop-and-how-to-spot-it)). Impeccable objects only to numbers that do not mark a real sequence ([Impeccable](https://impeccable.style/slop/)), and a clinical review is a real sequence.

Showing the work also has measured value. People valued services more when sites displayed the effort behind them, and some preferred a longer wait with visible work to an instant, identical result ([Buell & Norton](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.1110.1376)). In food-service experiments, letting customers and staff see each other raised customer-rated quality **22.2%** and cut throughput time 19.2% ([Buell, Kim & Tsay](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2449029)). It is plausible that this carries over to asynchronous doctor review, but nobody has tested it. The compliant way to use it is to describe what the reviewer checks.

**What you receive, what it costs and who it is not for carry most of the trust.** Certificate previews and employer verification are compliant, show no faces and are specific. Qoctor has a validity checker, Doccy a verify page, NextClinic a QR code and InstantScripts an example certificate ([Qoctor](https://www.qoctor.com.au/); [InstantScripts](https://www.instantscripts.com.au/online-medical-certificates)). An SMS eScript mock-up that shows no medicine works the same way ([NextClinic](https://nextclinic.com.au/prescriptions-online)).

Price works best as tinted tier cards, each with a one-line use case, as NextClinic and InstantScripts do. 21st.dev's pricing guidance adds three rules: compare options on the same rows, raise the option that suits most people rather than the most expensive one, and add no billing toggle unless billing actually varies ([21st.dev](https://21st.dev/blog/react-pricing-section-components)). In NN/g's credibility research, one user abandoned a site after 35 seconds because it did not state its rate ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/)).

Refund-on-decline belongs in this block as a stated term. Money-back guarantees raised purchase intention in retail experiments ([Suwelack et al.](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022435911000820)). In health, the safer framing is a policy with a link to its terms, avoiding "guaranteed" and "risk-free". That fits Ahpra's requirement that offer terms be clear and easy to find ([Ahpra](https://www.ahpra.gov.au/Resources/Advertising-hub/Advertising-guidelines-and-other-guidance/Advertising-guidelines.aspx)).

The most underused device is scope. NextClinic's not-for list names Centrelink, workers compensation, court, exam deferral and under-18s ([NextClinic](https://nextclinic.com.au/medical-certificates-online)). It sets expectations, heads off declines, and reads as clinical judgement rather than legal hedging. Linear and PostHog use the same idea in their what-we-don't-do sections ([Design Key](https://www.designkey.studio/post/saas-website-design-patterns-that-convert-2026)).

**Proof can come from facts and disclosure instead of testimonials.** Mercury pairs a customer count with a plain partner-bank disclosure and a section on how it protects accounts. Cash App opens a section with a scam-prevention figure and a zero-fraud-liability promise. Monzo puts consumer-group and deposit-protection marks at the hero ([Mercury](https://mercury.com); [Cash App](https://cash.app); [Monzo](https://monzo.com)). NN/g adds that people trust reviews on external sites more than those shown on a company's own site ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/trustworthy-design/)). That matches the linked ProductReview badge InstantMed already uses. Rating research is context only: purchase likelihood peaks between 4.0 and 4.7 stars and falls toward 5.0 ([Spiegel Research Center](https://spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu/how-online-reviews-influence-sales/)).

Two cautions apply. First, low proof can underperform no proof ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/5-landing-page-a/b-tests-and-their-surprising-results)). Live numbers such as a wait line or a patient total should render only when the number is strong, which InstantMed's hero rules already require ([DESIGN.md](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed-worktrees/landing-premium-plan/DESIGN.md)). Second, badges at the wrong step create anxiety. In an older test, removing a security badge from an opt-in form raised completion 12.6% ([HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/5-landing-page-a/b-tests-and-their-surprising-results)). Payment-security reassurance belongs at the payment step.

**Keep the FAQ short, close with one button, and add length only to answer objections.** FAQs should use the questions people actually ask, in their words, taken from support contacts ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/faqs-deliver-value/)). NextClinic's certificate FAQ runs past 20 questions and Qoctor's to 18 ([Qoctor](https://www.qoctor.com.au/medical-certificate/)). Six to eight visible questions with a link to the rest keeps a mobile page scannable. The close should be a single button on a contrasting band ([Evil Martians](https://evilmartians.com/chronicles/we-studied-100-devtool-landing-pages-here-is-what-actually-works-in-2025)).

In Unbounce's data, the best-converting healthcare pages carried **355–1,020 words** ([Unbounce](https://unbounce.com/conversion-benchmark-report/healthcare-wellness-conversion-rate/)). At a 390 px width, NextClinic covers price, process, scope and FAQ in about **9,100 px**. Qoctor's certificate page runs **18,221 px** and InstantConsult's homepage **24,095 px** ([InstantConsult](https://www.instantconsult.com.au/)). A Crazy Egg page 20 times longer than its control won by 30%, but only because every addition answered an objection found through research ([Conversion Rate Experts](https://www.conversion-rate-experts.com/crazy-egg-case-study/)). Users read at most 20–28% of the words on a page ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/)). They scan most efficiently by jumping from heading to heading ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/)), so each section heading should state its point as a claim.

**Sticky bars suit service pages and need testing on the homepage.** Sticky bottom CTAs are standard on UK and US service pages ([Numan ED](https://www.numan.com/erectile-dysfunction); [Manual ED](https://www.manual.co/erectile-dysfunction)). Across 33 A/B tests, sticky elements won most often at the bottom of the screen, on mobile and close to the transaction. They **never won on homepage-type pages** ([Online Dialogue](https://www.onlinedialogue.nl/en/blogs/sticky-cta-guaranteed-conversion-uplift/)). On the certificate and repeat pages, show the sticky CTA only after the hero action scrolls out of view. On the homepage, treat one as a test.

**Mobile needs its own composition.** At 375 px, a two-column bento grid stacks into a column of cramped boxes and loses its size hierarchy ([21st.dev](https://21st.dev/blog/react-bento-grid-components)). Paragraph-length content suits accordions; snap carousels suit only visual items. NN/g found scroll-jacking more disorienting on phones than on desktop ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/)).

## Australian rules narrow the words and pictures to checkable facts

### Three regulators set the lane

**Ahpra.** Section 133 of the National Law bars health advertising that is false or misleading, offers inducements without stating their terms, uses testimonials, creates an unreasonable expectation of benefit, or encourages unnecessary use. Penalties reach **A$60,000 per offence for an individual and A$120,000 for a company**, and Ahpra does not pre-approve advertising ([Ahpra](https://www.ahpra.gov.au/Resources/Advertising-hub/Advertising-guidelines-and-other-guidance/Advertising-guidelines.aspx)).

A statement becomes a testimonial when it refers to a clinical aspect: a symptom, a diagnosis or treatment, or an outcome. Comments about service or manner do not count. Independent review sites are unrestricted, but advertisers must remove clinical testimonials from platforms they control ([Ahpra testimonial tool](https://www.ahpra.gov.au/Resources/Advertising-hub/Resources-for-advertisers/Testimonial-tool.aspx)). Ahpra's summary page flags act-now and limited-time urgency as potentially unlawful when it is tied to claims that someone's health will suffer ([Ahpra summary](https://www.ahpra.gov.au/Resources/Advertising-hub/Advertising-guidelines-and-other-guidance/Summary-of-the-advertising-requirements.aspx)). "Free" must mean absolutely free, and titles such as "specialist" are reserved for practitioners with specialist registration.

**TGA.** The TGA's guidance of 18 June 2026 says that promoting a health service, telehealth included, as a way to obtain a specific prescription medicine is likely to be unlawful advertising. Indirect references and slang count. So do images that imply a medicine, such as syringes, pens, vials, capsules, pills or stylised pill logos ([TGA](https://www.tga.gov.au/resources/guidance/complying-restrictions-advertising-prescription-medicines-public); [TGA](https://www.tga.gov.au/resources/guidance/advertising-health-services-involve-therapeutic-goods)). Advertising Council Australia reports that the regulator now judges the overall impression a communication leaves, not only whether it names a product ([Advertising Council Australia](https://advertisingcouncil.org.au/news/new-tga-guidance-advertising-boundaries-for-prescription-medicines-health-services/)). The TGA enforces this: in 2024 it issued **21 infringement notices totalling A$319,260** to telehealth businesses and individuals over medicine advertising ([TGA](https://www.tga.gov.au/news/media-releases/telehealth-businesses-fined-over-300000-alleged-unlawful-advertising-weight-loss-medicines)).

**Medical Board of Australia.** The Board's telehealth guidelines, in force since September 2023, say that prescribing without a real-time consultation, including through an asynchronous questionnaire, is not good practice. The exception is the patient's usual practitioner ([Medical Board](https://www.medicalboard.gov.au/codes-guidelines-policies/telehealth-consultations-with-patients.aspx)). In October 2025 Ahpra renewed its concern about questionnaire-based prescribing and about business models focused on profit over safety ([Ahpra](https://www.ahpra.gov.au/News/2025-10-07-Updated-telehealth-guidance.aspx)). Competitors now advertise a real consultation as a differentiator ([Qoctor](https://www.qoctor.com.au/online-prescriptions/); [NextClinic](https://nextclinic.com.au/prescriptions-online)). For a form-first service, the defensible copy describes the doctor's review honestly, including the possibility that the doctor makes contact, and never sells "no call" as the benefit. InstantMed's own rules already say this ([CLAUDE.md](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed-worktrees/landing-premium-plan/CLAUDE.md)). This is a reading of the guidance, not legal advice.

InstantMed's house rules add limits that change specific copy ([CLAUDE.md](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed-worktrees/landing-premium-plan/CLAUDE.md)). Doctors are never named or counted. One rule changes a proof line many competitors use. The common reassurance that a doctor reviews every request is therefore accurate on the repeat-script page but not for every certificate. Certificate copy should use the approved, branch-aware claim wording.

### Copy that survives the skim and the regulator

Clarity beats cleverness in every source retrieved. MECLABS publishes case studies whose titles credit clarity changes with gains of about 200%; only the titles could be verified ([MarketingExperiments](https://marketingexperiments.com/value-proposition/clarity-trumps-persuasion)).

Harry Dry's three tests fit Ahpra's rules closely: can I visualise it, can I prove it false, and could nobody else say it ([Writing Examples](https://writingexamples.com/article/harry-dry-write-great-copy))? Facts that can be proved false pass both the copy test and the regulator: price, what arrives, how it arrives, who reviews it, and who is eligible. Unfalsifiable adjectives such as fast, easy, trusted or seamless give the reader nothing to check. Outcome promises can be proved false, but they break the rule against unreasonable expectation.

People who are unwell and on a phone read less than the desktop figures suggest, so the NHS standard is a sensible ceiling. It sets a reading age of 9 to 11, sentences of 20 words or fewer, paragraphs of three sentences or fewer, and the plain term first with the medical term in brackets ([NHS service manual](https://service-manual.nhs.uk/content/how-we-write)). Fernbach and colleagues found that some people want mechanistic detail, while others understand less and like a product less when it is explained in depth ([Journal of Consumer Research](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/39/5/1115/1794983)). The mechanism (form, review, secure link, eScript) therefore belongs in the steps and FAQ, not the H1.

At the moment of commitment, the button should describe the action plainly, in the first person where that reads naturally ([Copyhackers](https://copyhackers.com/how-to-optimize-your-button-copy/)). Beside it, a short line should answer the last worry ([Copyhackers](https://copyhackers.com/2012/06/101-copywriting-dos-and-donts/)). Mapped to Baymard's abandonment reasons, those worries are the all-in price, how long the form takes, what happens if the request is declined, privacy, and who makes the decision. NHS 111 shows warmth coming from plainness alone: a five-word task headline, one conditional sentence and first-person task links, with no adjectives ([NHS 111](https://111.nhs.uk/)).

NextClinic uses the Australian register consistently. The wit sits in section headers and microcopy, about staying in bed, tracking a request from your pillow, or being allowed to be sick. The clinical lines stay plain and conditional: if approved, if clinically appropriate, not charged if declined ([NextClinic](https://nextclinic.com.au/medical-certificates-online)). Scale boasts about being the largest or serving millions read as corporate, and 30–40-word paragraphs push the CTA below the first screen ([InstantScripts](https://www.instantscripts.com.au/); [Doctors on Demand](https://www.doctorsondemand.com.au/online-prescriptions/repeat-prescriptions/)). Craft slips are common even among premium brands. Ro and Headspace carry no H1, Manual and Software tag the wrong element as their H1, and one Australian certificate subhead has a grammar error ([Manual](https://www.manual.co/); [Doctors on Demand](https://www.doctorsondemand.com.au/online-medical-certificates/)).

A defensible budget, derived from these findings rather than tested:

| Element | Budget |
|---|---|
| H1 | 8 words or fewer |
| Supporting sentence | 16–20 words or fewer |
| Line beside the CTA | about 12 words |
| First line of each section | under 40 words |
| Visible body copy, excluding the FAQ | 200–300 words |

## Motion should confirm actions, never perform for attention

The practitioner consensus is narrow and consistent. Motion needs a job: feedback, a change of state, orientation, or occasionally deliberate delight ([Vercel](https://vercel.com/design/guidelines); [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-purpose-ux/)). It should be short. Emil Kowalski keeps UI animation under 300 ms and prefers a 180 ms select menu to a 400 ms one ([Emil Kowalski](https://emilkowal.ski/ui/7-practical-animation-tips)), and Rauno Freiberg caps interactions at 200 ms ([Rauno Freiberg](https://interfaces.rauno.me/)). Entrances should ease out, never ease in, animate only transform and opacity, and stay interruptible ([Emil Kowalski](https://emilkowal.ski/ui/great-animations)). Apple asks for brevity, optional motion and less animation on frequent interactions ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion)). IBM Carbon separates productive motion, which stays subtle, from expressive motion reserved for significant moments ([Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/motion/overview/)).

Kowalski concedes that marketing sites can afford more elaborate motion ([Emil Kowalski](https://emilkowal.ski/ui/you-dont-need-animations)). InstantMed's visitors are unwell, focused on a task and short of time, which puts them at the productive end.

| Motion job | Evidence range | Suggested InstantMed token |
|---|---|---|
| Press feedback | 70–120 ms; scale about 0.96–0.97 ([Carbon](https://carbondesignsystem.com/elements/motion/overview/); [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-duration/)) | 100–120 ms at the existing 0.98 scale |
| Hover colour or shadow | 200 ms or less, on hover-capable devices only ([Rauno Freiberg](https://interfaces.rauno.me/)) | 150 ms behind a hover media query |
| Entrance | 150–300 ms, decelerating, short travel; Vercel's reveal moves 10 px ([Next.js](https://nextjs.org/docs/app/guides/view-transitions)) | 200–250 ms, 8–12 px, a decelerate curve such as Material's `cubic-bezier(0.05, 0.7, 0.1, 1)` ([Material](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/material-components/material-components-android/master/docs/theming/Motion.md)) |
| Exit | Faster than the entrance ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-duration/)) | About 150 ms |
| Rare spatial change | 300–400 ms; Carbon's large expansion and Vercel's slide both take 400 ms | One 300–400 ms token, for intake step changes only |

No authoritative source gives a stagger interval; library examples use 10–100 ms ([Motion](https://motion.dev/docs/stagger)). For three or four items, 30–60 ms per item keeps the whole sequence under about 400 ms. That figure is an inference.

Motion that responds to the user reads as quality, and motion that moves on its own reads as slop. Hero entrances are safe only on elements that are not the LCP element, because of the paint rule described earlier. Word-by-word headline reveals delay the value proposition. NN/g found that text fading in gradually gets skipped and that scroll-triggered fades leave people thinking they have reached the end of the page. It advises avoiding scroll fading on mobile entirely ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scroll-fading-101/)). Number tickers are theatre unless the value is live; the NumberFlow component respects reduced motion by default ([NumberFlow](https://number-flow.barvian.me/)). Logo marquees fall under WCAG 2.2.2, which requires a pause control for content that moves on its own for more than five seconds ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)). Parallax was universally triggering for motion-sensitive participants in Val Head's research ([Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2020/09/design-reduced-motion-sensitivities/)). In NN/g's testing, people mistook scroll-jacking for a malfunction, and task-focused users left ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolljacking-101/)). Blurs and animated gradients cost GPU time on mid-range phones, because only transform and opacity are widely composited ([Motion](https://motion.dev/docs/performance)).

The accessibility position is to reduce motion, not remove it. WCAG 2.3.3 does not count opacity-only changes as motion animation, and honouring `prefers-reduced-motion` satisfies it ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)). Josh Comeau cites figures suggesting that up to 35% of US adults over 40 have experienced vestibular dysfunction; the primary study was not checked ([Josh W. Comeau](https://www.joshwcomeau.com/react/prefers-reduced-motion/)). The fix is to swap translate and scale for opacity at similar timing. Motion's `MotionConfig reducedMotion="user"` does this globally, disabling transform and layout animations while keeping opacity ([Motion](https://motion.dev/docs/react-accessibility)).

On the stack, the preference order is CSS first, then the Web Animations API, then JavaScript libraries ([Vercel](https://vercel.com/design/guidelines)). CSS scroll-driven animations run in Chrome 115+ and Safari 26 but not in stable Firefox, so they have to be progressive enhancement ([MDN browser-compat-data](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/main/css/properties/animation-timeline.json)). React's view transitions in Next 15.5 depend on the experimental React channel ([Next.js](https://nextjs.org/blog/next-15-2)), and landing pages do not need them. Motion's docs put the full `motion` component at 34 kB, against about 4.6 kB for the lazy `m` component plus 15 kB of features loaded on demand ([Motion](https://motion.dev/docs/react-reduce-bundle-size)). A local search of the InstantMed repository found 37 files importing framer-motion, none of them using `LazyMotion`, and no global `MotionConfig` reduced-motion wrapper ([repository](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed/components)).

No study found shows motion raising trust or conversion on health sites, and the widely repeated statistics claiming animation lifts conversion trace back to no primary source ([Attention Insight](https://attentioninsight.com/how-animations-can-improve-the-conversion-rate-of-your-website/)). For this audience, motion is a matter of hygiene.

InstantMed's rules are right for its visitors: durations of about 200–250 ms, no loops, no parallax, no rotation, one short hero entrance, and reduced motion always honoured. Two rules are stricter than the evidence requires. A chevron flipping 180° is feedback, not decoration. A single 300–400 ms token would suit rare spatial changes.

The real gaps are two documentation errors. DESIGN.md makes a stagger rise triggered on scroll (via `whileInView`) the default entrance for lists and card groups, against NN/g's advice for mobile. Its do/don't table also calls `initial={false}` an "invalid prop" ([DESIGN.md](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed-worktrees/landing-premium-plan/DESIGN.md)). Framer Motion 11.18.2's own type definitions document that prop as the way to disable the mount animation ([framer-motion types](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed/node_modules/framer-motion/dist/index.d.ts)), which makes it the simplest LCP guard for motion components above the fold.

The warm, premium brands studied do not stack visual devices. Each commits to one it owns and applies it in tiers.

Restraint around the chosen device is part of the effect. In March 2026, Linear warmed its greys, used fewer and smaller icons, and removed its coloured icon backgrounds ([Linear](https://linear.app/now/behind-the-latest-design-refresh)).

InstantMed's rules exclude the character-led routes, so what remains is object-led. That leaves inflated object icons like Wise's, and heavier marks reserved for the service tier as Airbnb does. It also leaves a closed shape grammar like Duolingo's: three rounded shapes, about 15 shapes per illustration, flat perspective and pill-shaped shadows only ([Duolingo](https://design.duolingo.com/illustration)). The last two routes are faceless still-life photography of the kind Mercury and NextClinic use, and texture; Canva reports texture searches up 30% year on year ([Canva](https://www.canva.com/newsroom/news/design-trends-2026/)).

Still life needs one exclusion from the TGA. NextClinic's prescriptions hero shows a medication box on a bedside table ([NextClinic](https://nextclinic.com.au/prescriptions-online)). Under the overall-impression test, even an unbranded pack is a risk.

Icon systems that look custom come from strict geometry and a closed vocabulary, whichever library they start from. Lucide's rules set a 24×24 canvas, a 2 px stroke, round joins and caps, a 2 px corner radius on shapes of 8 px or more, and at least 2 px between elements ([Lucide](https://lucide.dev/contribute/icon-design-guide)). For category tiles, Atlassian's icon tile runs from 20 to 48 px in ten hues, each with a subtle and a bold variant ([Atlassian](https://atlassian.design/components/icon/icon-tile/examples)). IBM Carbon sets a 48 px minimum for pictograms and allows only circle or square containers. It also bars pictograms from replacing UI icons and warns against overuse ([Carbon](https://v10.carbondesignsystem.com/guidelines/pictograms/usage/)). Atlassian reserves colourful spot illustrations for key moments and neutral ones for routine tasks, and warns that too much illustration adds cognitive load ([Atlassian](https://atlassian.design/foundations/illustrations)). Phosphor's duotone weight is a single colour at 20% opacity, so a true two-colour mark needs custom SVG ([Phosphor](https://github.com/phosphor-icons/react)).

Accessibility sets the colour limits. A glyph that carries meaning needs 3:1 contrast against the colour directly behind it, which is the tile when there is one ([W3C 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)). Colour coding is fine as long as text or shape backs it up ([W3C 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)). The research notes computed contrast for InstantMed's own token pairs:

| Pair | Contrast | Meaningful glyph (3:1)? |
|---|---|---|
| Brand coral #FF6B5B on page ivory #F8F7F4 | 2.61:1 | Fails |
| White glyph on solid coral | 2.80:1 | Fails |
| Coral glyph on a 10% coral tint | 2.37:1 | Fails |
| #2563EB (`--primary` in globals.css) on ivory | 4.82:1 | Passes |
| #2563EB on morning sky #BAD4F5 | 3.40:1 | Passes |
| #3B82F6 (labelled primary in DESIGN.md) on sky | 2.42:1 | Fails |
| #1D4ED8 (`--primary-strong`) on sky, peach or champagne | 4.30–4.62:1 | Passes |
| Slate-900 on any morning tint | 11.5–12.3:1 | Passes |
| Sky, peach or champagne tile against the ivory page | 1.35–1.46:1 | Tile edge barely visible |

Token sources: [DESIGN.md](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed-worktrees/landing-premium-plan/DESIGN.md), [globals.css](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed-worktrees/landing-premium-plan/app/globals.css).

The table has four consequences. **Coral cannot carry meaning in a small glyph**; it works as a large or decorative fill, as a tile colour behind a dark glyph, or next to a visible label. Tile glyphs need #2563EB or darker, and #1D4ED8 or slate is safe on every morning tint. Tiles need a hairline border or the sky-toned shadow to register as shapes against the ivory page. DESIGN.md and globals.css disagree on the primary blue, and one of the two values fails on sky, so that conflict needs resolving.

Each colour needs a written job. Blue is for action and navigation only. Coral is for brand moments and large fills. The morning spectrum is for service tiles and section fields, always with a dark glyph ink. A gradient appears once, as one restrained field. Headspace, Zocdoc, Monzo and One Medical each made their lead colour warm. InstantMed keeps blue for CTAs, so its warmth has to come from surfaces, marks and texture.

The current service tiles default to a tinted background with an outlined Lucide glyph, and the ED tile still sits on Tailwind's indigo scale, #6366F1 to #4F46E5 ([service-icons.tsx](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed-worktrees/landing-premium-plan/components/icons/service-icons.tsx)). DESIGN.md §7 still describes 135° gradient tiles with coloured shadows.

The production routes run from free to fully owned. Lucide plus a tile component costs nothing. Phosphor and Tabler are MIT-licensed, and Tabler has 6,220 icons ([Tabler](https://github.com/tabler/tabler-icons)). Hugeicons Pro costs US$99 a year ([Hugeicons](https://hugeicons.com/pricing)). Streamline costs US$19–29 a month and caps use at 100 icons or 50 illustrations per project ([Streamline](https://home.streamlinehq.com/pricing)). Rate guides put simple commissioned spot illustrations at US$50–500 each for limited digital rights, and commercial licences multiply fees 3–8 times ([FreelanceRates](https://www.freelancerates.net/illustrator)). A mid-market buyout of a 12–20-piece set plausibly lands around **US$6k–20k** over **4–8 weeks**, a range inferred from the guides rather than quoted. Purely AI-generated imagery has no copyright protection in the US or Australia ([US Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf); [Maddocks](https://www.maddocks.com.au/insights/whos-work-copyright-in-ai-generated-artwork)), and it struggles to hold perspective across a set ([Flarup](https://www.flarup.email/p/the-future-is-colourful-and-dimensional)). AI therefore suits exploration, with a human redrawing the finals. The cheapest credible route is Lucide for utility glyphs, plus six custom-drawn service marks that follow Lucide's grid, placed in a flat tile system.

## AI slop is now a named checklist, and InstantMed trips several items

The root cause has a name. Anthropic's Applied AI team called it *distributional convergence* in November 2025: models sample the safest, most common choices in their training data, such as Inter, purple gradients on white, and minimal animation ([Claude blog](https://www.claude.com/blog/improving-frontend-design-through-skills)). Tailwind's creator, Adam Wathan, joked that his choice of indigo-500 for Tailwind UI's buttons years ago is why AI interfaces come out indigo ([Adam Wathan on X](https://x.com/adamwathan/status/1953510802159219096)). The same drift is measurable in text. An analysis of more than 15 million PubMed abstracts estimated that at least **13.5%** of 2024 abstracts were processed with an LLM ([Kobak et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.07016)).

Critics' lists now overlap heavily ([Impeccable](https://impeccable.style/slop/); [Developers Digest](https://www.developersdigest.tech/blog/ai-design-slop-and-how-to-spot-it); [925Studios](https://www.925studios.co/blog/ai-slop-design-tells)). Colour and type tells come first: indigo or purple gradients, gradient text, coloured glows, glassmorphism, and Inter or Geist by default. Layout tells follow: a badge above the H1, a hero headline that fills the screen, identical cards topped with thin-line icons, rows of oversized metrics, auto-scrolling marquees and bento as the default feature layout. The rest are finishing habits: bounce easing and hover zooms, abstract 3D blobs, one radius and padding everywhere, and a single italic-serif accent word.

Anthropic's public frontend-design skill names five newer defaults ([Anthropic skill](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/anthropics/skills/main/skills/frontend-design/SKILL.md)). The first is a warm cream background near #F4F1EA with a high-contrast serif and a terracotta accent near #D97757. The second is near-black with a single acid accent, and the third is a broadsheet layout with hairline rules. The fourth is a SaaS card kit of identical rounded cards sharing one radius and one grey shadow. The fifth is *template chrome*: a letter-spaced label above every heading, middle-dot meta strings and an arrow on every link.

Health adds its own clichés: crosses and staffs, blue-grey palettes, stethoscopes, and generic or white-coat photography ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3056755/zocdocs-new-identity-puts-a-friendly-face-on-health-care); [It's Nice That](https://www.itsnicethat.com/articles/italic-studio-headspace-graphic-design-project-250424)). Corporate Memphis illustration and its clay-3D successor are widely treated as dated ([AIGA Eye on Design](https://eyeondesign.aiga.org/the-internet-is-turning-on-big-techs-colorful-corporate-mascots/)).

The reference galleries feed these defaults. Dribbble's popular results for telehealth landing pages listed 47 shots, dominated by one agency's GLP-1 concepts, with the top shot at 168k views ([Dribbble](https://dribbble.com/search/telehealth-landing-page)). The recurring elements are sage and cream palettes, large serif headlines, rating and stat cards, glass UI, vial and pen packshots, lifestyle portraits that often look AI-generated, and white-coat doctors surrounded by floating UI chips. Searches for "online doctor" return blue booking apps with stock doctors ([Dribbble](https://dribbble.com/search/online-doctor)). For an Australian service, these concepts are palette and type references at most. Their imagery is mostly unlawful, and the portraits break the rule against fake people.

Copy has its own tells. Wikipedia's maintained guide names inflated claims of significance, brochure vocabulary, shallow -ing clauses and vague attribution. It adds avoidance of a plain "is", not-X-but-Y constructions, reflexive groups of three, title-case headings, heavy bold and em-dash overuse ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing)). Marketing lists add words such as seamless, robust, unlock, elevate and empower ([ContentBeta](https://www.contentbeta.com/blog/list-of-words-overused-by-ai/)). Audiences notice. In IAB's January 2026 survey, Gen Z respondents were more than twice as likely as Millennials to call brands that use AI in ads inauthentic, at 30% against 13% ([IAB](https://www.iab.com/insights/the-ai-gap-widens/)).

These are signals rather than proof. Julian Shapiro warned against vague headers built on words like empower long before AI ([Julian Shapiro](https://www.julian.com/guide/growth/landing-pages)). The practical fix is a specificity test: every line should contain a fact only InstantMed could state.

The finding with the largest design consequence is that the premium DTC look of recent years has become the machine default. Cream grounds and soft serif display faces helped Hims, Juniper and One Medical look premium, and that combination now sits inside Anthropic's first cluster. InstantMed's rule against serifs protects it here by accident. Several other tells are live in its current system:

| Recognised tell | Where InstantMed shows it | Lower-risk alternative |
|---|---|---|
| Label above each heading (template chrome) | `SectionHeader` and `CenteredHero` expose a `pill` prop; the certificate page carries an eyebrow label and a FAQ pill ([DESIGN.md](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed-worktrees/landing-premium-plan/DESIGN.md); [med-cert-landing.tsx](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed-worktrees/landing-premium-plan/components/marketing/med-cert-landing.tsx)) | Let the heading state the claim; keep labels only where they mark a real sequence |
| Highlighted accent word or gradient text | `highlightWords` props; the new hero plan makes a blue closing phrase the hero signature ([hero plan](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed-worktrees/landing-premium-plan/docs/superpowers/plans/2026-09-26-landing-premium-heroes.md)) | Colour a fact (the deliverable or the price), or nothing |
| Identical cards with generic thin-line icons | Service tiles are a tinted background with an outlined Lucide glyph, and sections reuse stock check and badge glyphs ([service-icons.tsx](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed-worktrees/landing-premium-plan/components/icons/service-icons.tsx)) | One custom mark per service in a heavier tier; utility glyphs stay small and inline |
| Tailwind indigo default | ED tile on #6366F1 to #4F46E5 | Move it into the sky and blue family |
| Gradient tiles with coloured glows | Still specified in DESIGN.md §7, which has drifted from the code | Flat, low-saturation tints with dark glyphs and a neutral sky shadow |
| Animated metric counters | `StatsHero` with animated counters on the pricing and trust pages | Static, sourced numbers; animate only live values |
| Cream, serif and terracotta cluster | Ivory #F8F7F4 with coral #FF6B5B, without a serif | Keep the ground; tie coral to named moments and pair it with InstantMed's own artefacts |
| Shared default display face | Plus Jakarta Sans, the face NextClinic sets its 34 px, weight-700 H1 in ([NextClinic](https://nextclinic.com.au/)) | A distinctive weight, tracking and optical treatment, or a licensed characterful grotesk like Pilot's Right Grotesk |
| Scroll fade-ups everywhere | A stagger rise triggered on scroll is the default list entrance | No scroll reveals on mobile; content visible by default |
| AI-generated stock imagery | 41 generated photos, unused, some with garbled on-screen text and one showing a synthetic person ([hero plan](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed-worktrees/landing-premium-plan/docs/superpowers/plans/2026-09-26-landing-premium-heroes.md)) | Keep them out; use coded scenes or commissioned still life |

The remedy is Harry Dry's third test applied to design ([Writing Examples](https://writingexamples.com/article/harry-dry-write-great-copy)). That means artefacts only InstantMed can show (its certificate layout, its verification route, its eScript flow), rules only it follows (a custom mark grammar, colour jobs), and sentences only it can say (its price, its refund policy, its eligibility limits). One analysis of Stripe's many imitators reaches the same conclusion: copying a brand's form fails once that form is separated from its content ([Eleken](https://www.eleken.co/blog-posts/making-it-like-stripe)).

## A section-by-section blueprint for the three pages

The blueprint below follows from the evidence above. None of it has been tested on InstantMed traffic. The homepage works as a router. The certificate and repeat pages are simple offers for high-intent visitors, so their action belongs in the first screen. Prices and rules come from InstantMed's canonical documents ([CLAUDE.md](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed-worktrees/landing-premium-plan/CLAUDE.md)).

| Section | Homepage | Medical certificate | Repeat prescription |
|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | Names the services in plain nouns; no comparative claim | Opens with "medical certificate"; situational warmth goes in the sentence | Opens with "repeat prescription" or "repeat script"; signals a medicine you already take |
| First action | Six service rows (mark, one line, price) inside the first screen, as Hims, Pilot and NextClinic do | One CTA naming the request, with the price, from A$24.95 | One CTA naming the request, with A$29.95 |
| Beside the action | 24/7, AHPRA-registered doctors, refund if declined | Refund if declined; form time, if measured; no Medicare needed; 18+ | Refund if declined; Medicare with an IRN, or an IHI; the doctor may contact you |
| Visual | Specimen certificate and eScript token, shown in their real delivery channels | Specimen certificate (marked SPECIMEN, with a verification reference) as seen through the secure link | SMS token without a medicine name, using the real sender and wording |
| How it works | Three generic steps, linking to each service | Three steps: form; decision under the approved clinical model; secure link by email | Four steps: the medicine you take; health questions; doctor review, which may include contact; eScript token |
| Price | On each service row | Tinted 1-, 2- and 3-day cards (A$24.95, A$29.95, A$39.95), each with its use; priority as an optional A$9.95 add-on, with no timing claim | One card showing what is included; optional priority; no subscription |
| Scope | Link to the what-we-won't-do page | For work, study and carer's leave. Not for exam deferral, court, fitness-for-duty, workers compensation, NDIS or TAC, under-18s, or more than three days | For a medicine you already take. Not for new medicines, controlled medicines, or medicines that need a dedicated assessment (named by condition, never by drug) |
| Governance and proof | AHPRA-registered doctors, the Medical Director role, ABN and address, complaints route, privacy line, ProductReview badge and certification marks | As for the homepage, plus certificate verification | As for the homepage, plus a note that the prescription is the doctor's decision and repeats are at the doctor's discretion |
| FAQ | 4–6 routing questions | 6–8 real questions: employer acceptance (answered with Fair Work guidance, not promises), backdating, what the certificate shows, privacy, refunds, verification | 6–8 questions: Medicare or IHI, which medicines qualify, using the token at a pharmacy, repeats, whether the doctor will call, refunds |
| Close and sticky | One button to the service chooser; a sticky bar only as a test | One button; sticky bar appears after the hero action scrolls away | One button; the same sticky rule |

Price is why this matters commercially. Entry prices on the other Australian certificate pages ran from A$12.90 to A$19.95 ([Doccy](https://doccy.com.au/medical-certificate); [Hola](https://hola.health/medical-certificates/); [Qoctor](https://www.qoctor.com.au/medical-certificate/); [InstantScripts](https://www.instantscripts.com.au/online-medical-certificates); [NextClinic](https://nextclinic.com.au/medical-certificates-online); [Updoc](https://updoc.com.au/medical-certificate)). The exception was Doctors on Demand's after-hours consultation, from A$90 ([Doctors on Demand](https://www.doctorsondemand.com.au/online-medical-certificates/)). Ahpra treats price comparisons as high risk, so the A$24.95 price has to be justified by visible quality: the specimen, the verification route, the scope block, the refund terms, and governance a visitor can check.

### Where the evidence meets the current hero plan

The hero plan in this worktree already matches much of the evidence ([hero plan](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed-worktrees/landing-premium-plan/docs/superpowers/plans/2026-09-26-landing-premium-heroes.md)). It caps the hero at 32 words, keeps the price in the CTA, and builds coded outcome scenes. Its entrances are CSS-only, last 250 ms or less and never hold back the H1. It also sets a later budget of about 350 visible words of body copy, excluding FAQ answers. With the FAQ included, that lands inside the 355–1,020-word range where healthcare pages converted best. A few choices sit against the research.

| Plan choice | What the evidence says | Suggested adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| A coded phone showing the patient's outcome | Strongly supported: information-carrying visuals, real HTML over screenshots, NextClinic's SMS mock-up | Show the real channel. Certificates arrive as an emailed link to the patient dashboard, not as an attachment ([CLAUDE.md](file:///Users/rey/Developer/instantmed-worktrees/landing-premium-plan/CLAUDE.md)). ## Conclusion

Australia's advertising law and the conversion evidence point the same way. Once testimonials, faces, packshots and outcome statistics are off the table, the only proof left is proof a visitor can check: a price, a process, an artefact, a regulator fact or a refund term. That is also what plain-language research, the credibility studies and the 2026 anti-slop consensus reward. InstantMed's constraints amount to a brief for premium design. The pages meet it by filling that space with craft, not with stock template components.

The bigger shift is that style no longer signals premium on its own. The warm DTC look that set Hims and Juniper apart is now a default of the tools, and a model can reproduce it. What still signals premium is what neither a competitor nor a model can copy: documents only this service issues, rules only this brand follows, and facts only this business can state. By that test, InstantMed's coded outcome scenes and its refund and scope facts are assets it owns. Its current display face, its tinted icon cards and a waiting-room headline are not. The pages must also justify a certificate price above almost every competitor's. And since most UI changes produce no significant win, the redesign should ship in stages, each with a measure fixed in advance, and outside the Google Ads read windows.
